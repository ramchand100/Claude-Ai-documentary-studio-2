# Research Brief — EP001 — 1998: The Year Pakistan Went Broke on Purpose

**Status:** Draft — needs additional digging before script-ready (see Open Questions)
**Date:** 2026-08-30

## Background

In May 1998, Pakistan tested nuclear weapons for the first time, days after India tested its own. The tests triggered international economic sanctions almost immediately. Within days, the government took an extraordinary step: it froze all foreign currency bank accounts held by ordinary citizens and companies inside Pakistan — deposits people had legally saved in US dollars and other foreign currencies. Estimates put the frozen amount at up to $11 billion. The government's own reasoning was that it had to stop a "run" on the banks before the country's foreign exchange reserves ran out completely. By the end of that year, reserves fell to roughly $400 million — barely enough to cover a few weeks of imports. This episode explains how a national security decision (testing nuclear weapons) triggered a self-inflicted financial crisis, and why many older Pakistanis still don't fully trust keeping dollar savings in a Pakistani bank.

## History

- Pakistan's nuclear weapons program dates back to the 1970s, developed in response to India's 1974 "Smiling Buddha" test and the 1971 war.
- Through the 1990s, Pakistan already faced chronic foreign exchange pressure and was under a three-year IMF Enhanced Structural Adjustment Facility / Extended Fund Facility (ESAF/EFF) program agreed in October 1997, aimed at reducing the current account deficit and rebuilding reserves.
- At the program's six-month review in early 1998, the IMF assessed Pakistan's performance positively and continued the arrangement — meaning the economy was on a fragile but improving track *before* the tests.

## Different viewpoints

- **Government's position (1998):** The freeze was an unavoidable emergency measure — without it, a bank run would have exhausted reserves entirely and caused an uncontrolled default. Sartaj Aziz framed it as protecting the financial system as a whole over individual depositors' immediate access.
- **Depositors' position:** Many held these accounts specifically because they'd been encouraged (with tax and disclosure incentives) to bank foreign currency inside Pakistan rather than abroad. The freeze punished exactly the behavior the government had previously promoted, and eroded trust for years afterward — later account holders reportedly remained "distrustful of the future security of such deposits."
- **International/technical view:** IMF and rescheduling documents frame 1998 as a case where a geopolitical/security decision (the tests) overrode an economic program that was, on its own terms, showing modest improvement — i.e., the crisis was avoidable and self-inflicted relative to the pre-test trajectory. This is the angle the working title ("...on Purpose") leans on and should be handled carefully — see sensitivity note below.

## Open questions / gaps

- **Exact date of the FCA freeze announcement** — sources confirm "May 1998, following the nuclear tests" but not the specific day. Needs a primary/contemporaneous news archive check (e.g. Dawn's own 1998 archive) before the script states a date on screen.
- **GDP growth figures are unverified** (flagged above) — need PBS or SBP annual report figures for FY1997-98 and FY1998-99 specifically, not calendar-year approximations from a secondary summary.
- **What ultimately happened to the frozen accounts** — sources describe the freeze and the resulting distrust, but not the resolution mechanism (were depositors eventually paid in rupees, in installments, in dollars, or partially compensated via special bonds?). This is likely the single most important human-interest detail for the script's ending and needs a dedicated search pass.
- **Sanctions' concrete economic bite** — confirmed the US Glenn Amendment and Japan's aid suspension were imposed, but exact dollar figures of aid/loans cut off would strengthen the "why reserves collapsed" section.
- Consider whether to extend the episode's timeline into 1999 (Kargil conflict, October 1999 coup) as a coda on lasting consequences, or keep strictly to the 1998-99 financial crisis — a structural decision for the Documentary Structure skill, not resolved here.

## Sensitivity flag (for `SENSITIVITY_CHECKLIST.md`)

This episode touches nuclear weapons policy and a specific government's economic decision-making. The working title's framing ("on purpose") is journalistically defensible — the tests were a deliberate choice with foreseeable economic consequences — but the script must be careful to state that the *tests* were deliberate and the *economic fallout* was a foreseeable-but-not-intended consequence, not imply the government deliberately impoverished citizens. Keep the account-freeze framing factual (what happened, whose decision, stated justification) rather than assigning motive.
