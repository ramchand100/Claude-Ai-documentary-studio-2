**Status:** Draft — needs additional digging before script-ready (see Open Questions)

## Background

A basic small car costs multiples in Pakistan what the identical model costs in a neighboring market. The Suzuki Alto — one of the most common small cars on Pakistani roads — starts at roughly PKR 2.99-3.3 million in Pakistan, while the same model in India starts around Rs 3.25 lakh (with the top variant around Rs 5.12 lakh) — commonly cited as roughly a 7-8x price gap. This episode explains why: decades of tariff-based protectionism designed to build a local manufacturing industry, a market long dominated by three assemblers ("the Big Three" — Suzuki, Toyota, Honda), and a tax structure that piles customs duty, regulatory duty, and sales tax onto both imported and locally-assembled vehicles. It's also a live story right now — Pakistan is mid-way through an IMF-backed tariff reform (2025-2030) that is actively cutting these duties, making "is this finally changing?" a genuine, current-tense question rather than settled history.

## History

- **1982:** Pakistan's automotive manufacturing sector begins with the establishment of a car assembly plant.
- **1987-2004 — Deletion Program (Localization Policy):** Mandatory, progressively increasing local-parts-content requirements for assemblers, aimed at building a domestic vendor/parts industry and reducing import dependence. Built a local vendor base but did not produce export-competitive manufacturing.
- **2006 — Tariff-Based System (TBS):** Replaced the Deletion Program to comply with WTO Trade-Related Investment Measures rules. Kept high customs duties on parts already produced locally, while allowing non-localized parts to be imported at lower (CKD-equivalent) rates — reinforcing the position of already-established assemblers rather than opening the market.
- **2016-2021 — Automotive Industry Development Policy (AIDP):** Aimed at breaking the "Big Three" dominance and attracting new investment/new entrants; considered comparatively successful at increasing competition.
- **2021-2026 — Auto Industry Development and Export Policy (AIDEP):** Shifted focus toward exports, technology adoption, and sustainability; added incentives for hybrid and battery-electric vehicles.
- **2025-2030 — National Tariff Policy (current, IMF-backed):** Targets cutting Pakistan's weighted average tariff from 10.6% to 6% by 2030. For autos specifically: Completely Built Unit (CBU) import tariffs cut from 20% to 15% over five years; regulatory duties cut 55-90% in the first year alone; additional customs and regulatory duties on autos slated for full elimination by 2030; used-vehicle import tariffs start at a 40% surcharge above new-vehicle rates in FY26, declining 10 percentage points/year to reach parity by 2030; hybrid vehicles get a 25% customs duty reduction, pure EVs a 50% reduction (EVs also keep a concessionary 1% sales tax and CKD customs exemptions locked in through 30 June 2027).

## Different viewpoints

- **Industry/protectionist view:** Decades of tariff protection were necessary to build any domestic manufacturing base at all — without it, a country the size of Pakistan's auto market risked being flooded by fully-built imports from larger, more efficient markets (India, Thailand, etc.), and no local parts/vendor industry or jobs would ever have developed. PAMA has lobbied specifically to preserve incentives for small cars, HEVs and BEVs even as broader tariffs come down.
- **Consumer/reform-critical view:** The protection enriched a small number of assemblers (the "Big Three" oligopoly) without ever delivering the promised outcomes — genuine localization depth, export competitiveness, or lower prices — for decades. Press coverage explicitly frames this as the industry "fumbling" repeated five-year policy cycles and Pakistan being unable to "break its auto import addiction." This is the critical framing already present in respected industry press (Profit by Pakistan Today, The News), not just an assumption — good ammunition for a balanced "did protection work?" section.
- **Reform/IMF view:** The current tariff cuts are framed as a necessary correction — reducing tariffs and regulatory duties toward global norms, with the explicit goal of lower consumer prices, though the auto industry itself has raised concerns (per Arab News headline: "IMF-backed tariff reforms raise concerns for Pakistan's auto industry despite rising car sales") that need to be represented, not dismissed.
