## Background

A basic small car costs multiples in Pakistan what the identical model costs in a neighboring market. The Suzuki Alto — one of the most common small cars on Pakistani roads — starts at roughly PKR 2.99-3.3 million in Pakistan, while the same model in India starts around Rs 3.25 lakh (with the top variant around Rs 5.12 lakh) — commonly cited as roughly a 7-8x price gap. This episode explains why: decades of tariff-based protectionism designed to build a local manufacturing industry, a market long dominated by three assemblers ("the Big Three" — Suzuki, Toyota, Honda), and a tax structure that piles customs duty, regulatory duty, and sales tax onto both imported and locally-assembled vehicles. It's also a live story right now, more so than initially found — Pakistan is not just mid-reform but literally between policies: the old five-year auto policy expired 30 June 2026, and a successor (2026-31) is still being negotiated inside government as of this research, with an actual unresolved dispute between the Ministry of Commerce (wants faster tariff cuts) and the Ministry of Industries and Production (wants a slower path). "Is this finally changing, and how far?" isn't just a framing device — it's a genuinely open question with real stakeholders arguing about the answer right now.

## History

**Note:** the sub-points below now incorporate direct extraction from the CCP's own February 2026 report, which uses a slightly different four-phase periodization (i. Developing period 1947-1972; ii. Nationalization of Industries 1973-1987; iii. Privatization of Industries 1987-1995; iv. Post-privatization 1995-present) alongside the trade-policy-mechanism timeline below — the two aren't contradictory, just two different lenses (ownership structure vs. tariff mechanism) on the same history.

- **1982:** Pakistan's automotive manufacturing sector begins with the establishment of a car assembly plant. Suzuki's early 800cc model became a foothold affordable option for middle-income consumers; the lineup later expanded (Khyber 1000cc, Margalla 1300cc), entrenching Suzuki's dominance early.
- **1987-2004 — Deletion Program (Localization Policy):** Mandatory, progressively increasing local-parts-content requirements for assemblers. **Per the CCP's own report: achieved real indigenization — 56-70% in cars, 81-88% in motorcycles — but stayed limited to lower-value components; high-value/advanced components (engines etc.) remained import-dependent.** Built a local vendor base but did not produce export-competitive manufacturing.
- **2006 — Tariff-Based System (TBS):** Replaced the Deletion Program to comply with WTO Trade-Related Investment Measures rules. **CCP's own figures: introduced a differentiated structure — CBU import tariffs of 50% to over 100% depending on engine size, lower duties on CKD kits to favor local assembly, and 0-10% on raw materials/inputs for registered vendors.** This is the direct source of the "up to 155%" figure found in an earlier search pass — it's a real, if extreme, point within this range for certain large engine sizes, not a separate/contradictory figure.
- **2016-2021 — Automotive Industry Development Policy (AIDP):** Aimed at breaking the "Big Three" dominance and attracting new investment/new entrants; considered comparatively successful at increasing competition. This is when Hyundai Nishat Motors and Kia Lucky Motors were established (both founded 2017; Kia's plant operational September 2019, Hyundai's locally-assembled cars available from early 2020) — both entered specifically under ADP 2016-21 incentives.
- **2021-2026 — Auto Industry Development and Export Policy (AIDEP):** Shifted focus toward exports, technology adoption, and sustainability; added incentives for hybrid and battery-electric vehicles.
- **2025-2030 — National Tariff Policy — DISCREPANCY NOW RESOLVED (2026-08-31, via direct read of the primary NTP 2025-30 document, obtained through Tavily's extraction tool after the Ministry of Commerce site's Cloudflare block had stopped WebFetch):** Both figures found earlier were correct — they're just different metrics from the same document. **20.19% → 9.70% by 2029-30 is the *simple average* tariff rate** (Table 1 of the NTP document: Current/Base Year 2024-25 = 20.19% combining CD 11.93 + ACD 3.66 + RD 4.60, declining to 9.70% by Year V). **10.6% → below 6% is the *trade-weighted average*** — the document states this explicitly: "The reduction in tariff rates will bring the trade weighted average from the current 10.6% to below 6% in a period of 5 years." Both targets are real and both are in the same document; the script should specify which metric it's citing rather than treating them as competing figures. This is Pakistan's *second* National Tariff Policy, building on NTP 2019-24 (which took the effective applied average tariff from 10.6% in 2018-19 down to 6.7% by 2023-24). A new **Tariff Policy Board (TPB)**, chaired by the Minister/Advisor for Commerce with Industries & Production, Finance, Revenue (FBR), Board of Investment, and Planning Commission all represented, now oversees tariff policy. **Regional context confirmed in the same document:** Pakistan's simple average MFN tariff (10.3%) plus ACD (3.65%) and RD (4.57%) makes its total protection the highest in South Asia — higher than India (17.0% MFN alone, but India's total stack wasn't given for direct comparison), Bangladesh (14.1%), Nepal (12.7%), Bhutan (9.3%), and Sri Lanka (8.4%); China sits at 7.5%, ASEAN average 6.49%, global average 8.8%. **This directly informs the still-open "India price comparison fairness caveat"** — Pakistan's tariff stack being the highest in the region is real, sourced context for *why* the same car costs so much more here, not just an assumption. For autos specifically, the document confirms: **AIDEP 2021-26 is "valid till June 2026," with the new auto policy to be introduced "from first July 2026,"** including review of three named regulatory orders (SRO 655(I)/2006, SRO 656(I)/2006, SRO 693(I)/2006) and elimination of all auto-sector ACDs and RDs; a footnote notes some auto products currently sit at 35% CD, with auto-sector ACDs "eliminated gradually from July 1, 2026." A companion **New Energy Vehicle (NEV) Policy 2025-30** sets specific volume targets across vehicle categories (2/3/4-wheelers, buses/vans, trucks/LCVs), totaling just over 2.2 million NEVs targeted cumulatively by 2029-30.
- **2026-2031 — New Automobiles and Auto Parts Manufacturing Policy (AIDEP 2026-31) — CURRENT, UNRESOLVED, developing as this brief is written:** The previous AIDEP 2021-26 **expired 30 June 2026** — meaning Pakistan is, as of this research (31 Aug 2026), in a policy gap between the old and new frameworks. The successor policy is part of Pakistan's commitments under the IMF's $7 billion Extended Fund Facility. It is meant to take effect **1 July 2026** (already passed, but the policy itself was still not finalized as of the most recent sourcing) and targets over 500,000 vehicles produced per year and $1 billion in auto exports by 2031. **There is an active, unresolved dispute between government bodies over how far to cut tariffs**: the Ministry of Commerce's National Tariff Policy is pushing for CBU import duties as low as 15% by 2030, while the Ministry of Industries and Production has proposed a slower path — tariffs between 40% and 75% by 2030, depending on engine capacity. An Ishaq Dar-led steering committee reportedly reached "in-principle" consensus around 13 August 2026, with the proposal then going to the Ministry of Industries and Production and on to federal cabinet — **not yet formally cabinet-approved as of this research pass.** This is a live, fast-moving story — verify current status again close to publish/script-lock, since it could resolve (or shift again) before this episode airs.

## Different viewpoints

- **Industry/protectionist view:** Decades of tariff protection were necessary to build any domestic manufacturing base at all — without it, a country the size of Pakistan's auto market risked being flooded by fully-built imports from larger, more efficient markets (India, Thailand, etc.), and no local parts/vendor industry or jobs would ever have developed. PAMA has lobbied specifically to preserve incentives for small cars, HEVs and BEVs even as broader tariffs come down.
- **Consumer/reform-critical view:** The protection enriched a small number of assemblers (the "Big Three" oligopoly) without ever delivering the promised outcomes — genuine localization depth, export competitiveness, or lower prices — for decades. Press coverage explicitly frames this as the industry "fumbling" repeated five-year policy cycles and Pakistan being unable to "break its auto import addiction." This is the critical framing already present in respected industry press (Profit by Pakistan Today, The News), not just an assumption — good ammunition for a balanced "did protection work?" section.
- **Reform/IMF view:** The current tariff cuts are framed as a necessary correction — reducing tariffs and regulatory duties toward global norms, with the explicit goal of lower consumer prices, though the auto industry itself has raised concerns (per Arab News headline: "IMF-backed tariff reforms raise concerns for Pakistan's auto industry despite rising car sales") that need to be represented, not dismissed.
- **Live inter-government dispute (as of Aug 2026):** This isn't just industry-vs-consumer — it's playing out *inside* the government right now. The Ministry of Commerce (via the National Tariff Policy) wants CBU tariffs down to 15% by 2030; the Ministry of Industries and Production wants a slower path, 40-75% depending on engine size. A PakWheels analysis frames the emerging winners/losers: consumers and EV/high-tech vendors and formal importers stand to gain from faster liberalization; weak/less-competitive local assemblers and informal "grey-market" importers stand to lose. One line from that analysis is a strong episode thesis candidate: **"tariff cuts must reach the buyer, not disappear into OEM margins"** — i.e., even if duties fall, there's no guarantee assemblers pass the savings on to consumers.
