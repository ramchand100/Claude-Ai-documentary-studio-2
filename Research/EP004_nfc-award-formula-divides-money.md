## Background

Pakistan's four provinces don't collect most of the tax revenue that funds them — the federal government does, then redistributes it. How that money gets split between the centre and the provinces, and *among* the provinces, is decided by the National Finance Commission (NFC) Award — a formula, renegotiated periodically, that most Pakistanis have never heard of but that shapes how much money every provincial government has for schools, hospitals, and roads. The current formula has been frozen in place since 2009 — 16 years without a genuine update — while a new commission meant to revise it has spent the last year struggling to even hold its meetings. This episode explains what the formula actually does, why it hasn't changed in so long, and what's happening (or not happening) right now.

## History

- **1973 Constitution, Article 160:** Establishes the National Finance Commission — a body the President must constitute periodically to decide how federal tax revenue (the "divisible pool") is split between the federation and the provinces, and among the provinces themselves. Part of Chapter 1, Part VI of the Constitution.
- **1973–2009:** For over three decades, **population was the sole criterion** for splitting money among the provinces. This structurally favored the most populous province (Punjab) and was a long-standing grievance for Sindh, KP, and Balochistan.
- **2001 and 2006 NFC commissions:** Both failed to reach consensus — no award was announced from either.
- **7th NFC Award — signed 30 December 2009, effective 1 July 2010:** The breakthrough award. For the first time, moved beyond population-only distribution to a **multi-criteria formula**: Population 82%, Poverty/Backwardness 10.3%, Revenue Collection/Generation 5% (split 2.5%/2.5%), Inland Area (inverse population density) 2.7%. This directly answered long-standing demands from Sindh (wanted revenue generation counted — it benefits from being the country's main port), KP (wanted poverty/backwardness weighted), and Balochistan (wanted area counted, given its large land area and small population). The provinces' combined share of the divisible pool also rose — from 47.5% to 56% in 2010-11, then to 57.5% from 2011 onward.
- **18th Constitutional Amendment:** Added Article 160(3A) — a "non-reduction" guarantee: no future NFC Award can reduce any province's share below what it received in the *previous* award. This is a significant structural protection worth explaining, since it changes the political stakes of any future negotiation (provinces can only gain or hold steady, never lose ground, at least on paper).
- **7th Award's five-year term expired in 2015.** No consensus was reached on a successor for a decade. **The 7th Award's formula has continued to apply by default in the absence of a new one** — the exact constitutional/legal mechanism for this continuation needs a precise citation before stating it on screen (see Open Questions), but the practical effect is clear: Pakistan has been distributing money under a 2009 formula for 16+ years.
- **22 August 2025 — 11th NFC constituted:** President Asif Ali Zardari formed the 11th National Finance Commission, a nine-member body chaired by Finance Minister Muhammad Aurangzeb, with all four provincial finance ministers as permanent members plus one technical nominee per province.
- **30 August 2025:** Balochistan revised its technical nominee (to Mahfooz Ali Khan) — a minor but concrete sign of how much provincial input goes into even the commission's composition.
- **Inaugural session postponed** at Sindh's request due to that year's flood situation.
- **4 December 2025 — first actual meeting held.** Eight technical working groups were subsequently notified (covering: vertical resource distribution, national debt composition, improving the tax-to-GDP ratio, direct provincial transfers, horizontal distribution criteria, and federal expense-sharing in areas that are constitutionally provincial domains).
- **January 2026 — second commission meeting postponed.** As of the most recent reporting found, only 2 of the 8 working groups had met even once; the other 6 hadn't met at all since being notified in December 2025. Federal and provincial sides publicly blamed each other for the delays (federal side cited the Finance Minister's and Finance Secretary's overseas commitments; provincial representatives said federal absence was itself the cause of delay).
- **February 2026 — federal Planning Minister Ahsan Iqbal** publicly called for "comprehensive, forward-looking reform" of the NFC framework — notably, a federal government figure himself questioning the population-heavy formula, not just the provinces.

## Different viewpoints

Representing each province's actual stated position, not a paraphrase through another's lens — this is the section that most needs care per the sensitivity flag above:

- **Sindh's position:** Successfully pushed for "revenue collection/generation" to be counted in the 7th Award (5% weight) — this criterion structurally benefits Sindh as home to Pakistan's main port and largest share of federal tax collection. Sindh also requested the postponement of the 11th NFC's inaugural session due to flooding in the province — a scheduling fact, not a policy stance, but worth including for accuracy.
- **Khyber Pakhtunkhwa's position:** Has historically pushed for poverty and "backwardness" to be weighted more heavily — this benefited KP directly in the 7th Award's design. KP's Finance Minister leads the 11th NFC's working group specifically on the financial impact of the former FATA's 2018 merger into KP, an ongoing fiscal burden specific to that province.
- **Balochistan's position:** Has pushed to reduce population's dominance in the formula (proposed cutting it from 82% to 57%, per a flagged/unconfirmed figure) and increase the weight given to land area (from 2.7% to 15%) — Balochistan is Pakistan's largest province by area but has a comparatively small population, so a population-heavy formula structurally limits its share regardless of its land, resource, or development needs.
- **Punjab's position:** Not directly sourced in this research pass — as the most populous province, Punjab is the structural beneficiary of the current population-heavy (82%) weighting, which is a reasonable inference but **should not be stated as Punjab's explicit position without a direct source**; flagged as an open question.
- **Federal government's position:** Notably not monolithic — Finance Minister Aurangzeb chairs the commission tasked with reform, while Planning Minister Ahsan Iqbal has separately and publicly called the population-heavy formula into question. This suggests the federal government itself doesn't have one settled internal view, which is worth representing rather than flattening into a single "federal vs. provincial" framing.
- **Structural argument (from research literature, e.g. PIDE, SDPI):** A population-heavy formula may create a perverse incentive — provinces have less fiscal reason to slow population growth or invest in family planning, since population size directly drives their revenue share. This is a documented academic critique (see "The Role of NFC Award in Incentivizing Population Growth in Pakistan"), not a partisan talking point, and can be presented as a structural/technical critique rather than attributed to any one province.
