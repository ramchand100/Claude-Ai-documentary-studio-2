## Background

Since 1959, provincial governments have required that school textbooks come from officially-created "textbook boards" — a system that started as a quality-control measure and became, in practice, a near-monopoly. Private publishers can produce textbooks, but only after paying steep approval fees and passing content review — and the curriculum itself changes often enough (most dramatically with the 2021 Single National Curriculum, and again with a 2026-27 Punjab overhaul currently underway) that "last year's book" regularly becomes unusable, forcing families to buy new copies every year. This episode traces the system from its 1959 origin through a documented 2020 book-banning scandal to the live curriculum overhaul happening as this is written — using it to explain a quiet, recurring cost that hits nearly every schooling household in the country.

## History

- **5 January – 26 August 1959 — the Sharif Commission:** Formally the "Commission on National Education," chaired by S.M. Sharif (a former teacher of Ayub Khan). Concluded that textbook production was too complex to leave to individual authors/publishers and recommended dedicated government "textbook boards" as the sole authorized source of school textbooks per province/region.
- **Result:** East Pakistan and West Pakistan Textbook Boards established first; after the 1970 abolition of the "One Unit" policy, each reconstituted province formed its own board (Punjab Curriculum and Textbook Board, Sindh Textbook Board, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa Textbook Board, Balochistan Textbook Board).
- **Long-run effect:** With competition eliminated, boards became monopolies criticized for mistake-filled content, poor pedagogy, and low print quality — a criticism that predates any specific recent scandal and appears to be a structural, long-standing complaint.
- **10 August 2020 — the book-banning incident (Punjab):** PCTB Managing Director Manzoor Hussain banned roughly 100 textbooks as "against the two-nation theory" or "unethical and illegal." Around 10,000 books from 31 publishers were seized — including books from major international publishers Oxford and Cambridge, plus local publishers Link International Pakistan and Paragon Books. Hussain was subsequently removed from his post; an unnamed board official told Dawn that upset publishers had influenced the removal, aiming to get a more favorable replacement installed.
- **2021 — Single National Curriculum (SNC):** A major curriculum standardization push. Punjab implemented it with a costly, multi-layered private-publisher approval process (see Numbers); **Sindh rejected the SNC outright**, keeping publisher autonomy; **KP adopted a lighter version**, skipping the religious-review layer Punjab required.
- **2026-27 — Punjab curriculum overhaul (live, current):** 29 textbooks for classes 6 through matric sanctioned for replacement, effective the 2026-27 academic year. A new body, PECTAA, is replacing the Punjab Curriculum and Textbook Board, the Quaid-i-Azam Academy for Educational Development, and the Punjab Examination Commission — centralizing curriculum, teacher training, and assessment under one administration. Publishers say they weren't consulted and estimate losses around Rs 2 billion; the provincial School Education Minister said consultation wasn't required for curriculum decisions.

## Different viewpoints

- **Original policy rationale (1959):** Textbook production is too important and complex for private authors/publishers to handle unsupervised — a dedicated government body ensures quality and consistency. This was the Sharif Commission's stated reasoning, not an invented strawman.
- **Publisher/market view:** The approval-fee system (NOC fees, 7.5% royalty, multi-layer review) is a rent-seeking barrier that inflates prices without improving quality — and abrupt curriculum changes (2020 book bans, the 2026-27 overhaul) are imposed without real consultation, causing real financial losses to a private industry that exists legally and serves a real market need.
- **Government/board view:** Curriculum changes serve pedagogical goals — Punjab's 2026-27 minister framed the changes as reducing "unnecessary content" and improving learning outcomes, and stated consultation isn't legally required for curriculum decisions. Content review (including the religious-review layer) exists to screen for "anti-Pakistan, anti-religion, or other hate material."
- **Provincial divergence as its own argument:** Sindh's choice to reject the SNC and let publishers operate with more autonomy is a natural real-world comparison — it demonstrates the system doesn't have to work the way Punjab's does, without the episode needing to declare a winner.
