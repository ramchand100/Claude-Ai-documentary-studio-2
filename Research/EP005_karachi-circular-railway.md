# Research Brief — EP005 — The Karachi Circular Railway: The Train That's Been "Coming Soon" for 30 Years

**Status:** Draft
**Date:** 2026-09-01

## Background

Karachi is Pakistan's largest city — **20,382,881 people** per the official 2023 census (Pakistan Bureau of Statistics, Tier 1). It runs almost entirely on road transport: roughly **4.1 million registered vehicles**, growing by about 28,000 a month (quote attributed to former Sindh Transport Minister Syed Nasir Shah, Tier 3, not independently verified against a primary document). The city once had a working urban rail loop — the Karachi Circular Railway (KCR), a 43-44km loop around the city with roughly two dozen stations — that carried up to 6 million passengers a year at its peak. It has been effectively shut since 1999, except for one small revived segment.

Since then, KCR has been the subject of at least 14 distinct, named, publicly-announced revival attempts spanning four different governments and three different would-be financiers (Japan's JICA, China under CPEC, and now the Asian Development Bank) — and yet, as of this research (Sept 2026), the only track actually running is a single 14km partial segment reopened in 2020. This episode's spine is that repeated pattern itself: not just "what is KCR," but *why does a fully-approved, repeatedly-funded infrastructure project never actually get built*.

## History

**Origins (founding year — sourcing conflict, see flag below):** Most-repeated figure across Tier 2-3 sources (Dawn, C40 Cities case study, railway-technology.com) is that KCR began service in **1964** under President Ayub Khan. A single lower-tier source (LinkedIn essay) claims the concept dates to 1962 with first train running 1969 and the full loop completing 1970. **Recommend using 1964 as the on-screen figure**, since it's the one repeated across independent Tier 2/3 sources, but this isn't from a single authoritative primary document — treat as "well-corroborated," not "certain."

- **Original route:** Karachi City Station to Drigh Road Station, later extended to Landhi Junction (1970) and built out into the full loop through the 1970s.
- **Length/scale:** 43.13-44km depending on the year measured (JICA's own 2016-17 survey — the most authoritative figure available — states **43.24 km total**: 15.68km on-ground, 23.86km elevated, 2.28km in trench, 1.42km bridge). **24 stations** (JICA figure: 10 on-ground, 12 elevated, 2 in trench) is the best-supported station count — some older sources say 16, likely referring to a sub-section rather than the full loop.
- **Peak operation:** 104 daily trains (80 main-line, 24 loop), ~6 million passengers/year (~16,400/day average) — Tier 3, cross-corroborated across multiple outlets.
- **Decline:** Losses widened through the 1990s as operating costs outpaced fare revenue, alongside deteriorating maintenance and reported collusion between corrupt railway staff and private bus/van operators who benefited from diverting KCR's ridership onto the road network (this specific collusion claim is Tier 3/4, repeated across several outlets but not independently document-verified — attribute it as "reported," not stated flat). Service cuts began in **1994** (loop-only service continued); Pakistan Railways **fully suspended operations by December 1999**, citing unsustainable losses.
- **Notable and unverified anecdote:** Multiple lower-tier sources describe a mid-1990s episode where Karachi's municipal corporation used freight cars on the idle loop track to haul city garbage ("kooRa train") to dumping sites near Pakistan Steel Mills, abandoned after rural dumping became politically controversial. This is a good documentary beat but is **Tier 4/5-sourced (search-snippet synthesis, not a directly-fetched primary article)** — use with explicit "reportedly" attribution if included, or verify further before stating as fact.

## Different viewpoints

- **JICA vs. Sindh government (2013-2016):** JICA wanted encroachments cleared before committing financing, partly out of genuine concern about the human displacement a clearance operation would cause. The Sindh government wanted firm financing commitments locked in before it would move against thousands of encroaching households — a politically costly step to take on spec. Neither side was willing to move first; this standoff is the direct, sourced cause of JICA's 2016 withdrawal (Herald/Dawn, directly read). Represent both sides' actual stated reasoning — this isn't a case of one side being obviously in the wrong.
- **Federal government vs. Sindh government (recurring, 2016-2024):** Pakistan Railways (federal) legally owns the KCR land/right-of-way; the operational and political benefit accrues mostly to Karachi/Sindh. The core disagreement: Sindh wanted federal land transferred at a nominal Re. 1/sq yard rate; the federal government wanted Sindh to pay for the land, arguing normal acquisition terms should apply. Sindh's counter-argument is that the land originally belonged to Sindh. This dispute recurred as recently as 2024 reporting (headline-confirmed). This is a genuine structural "nobody wants to be first to pay/act" mechanism, not a one-off disagreement — good spine material for explaining *why* revival keeps stalling rather than just *that* it stalls.
- **Displaced residents:** Not independently sourced in this research pass beyond the fact that a review petition was filed against the 2018 anti-encroachment operation, and that roughly 4,000-4,600 households were affected. Their specific grievances/perspective were not found in the sources gathered — **flag this as a gap**, similar to how EP004 flagged Punjab's unsourced position on the NFC formula. If this angle matters for the episode, it needs more targeted research (human-interest reporting on the 2018 operation specifically) before scripting.
- **Pakistan Railways' internal skepticism:** At least one account (single-sourced) describes senior PR officials as privately skeptical that the Sindh government has the technical capacity to run a modern rail project, even as the political/asset-transfer negotiations proceeded. Worth attributing carefully given the single-source status.
